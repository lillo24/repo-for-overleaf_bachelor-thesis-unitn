The quantitative analysis supports three related conclusions: rewriting consistently improves retrieval, the size of the improvement varies moderately across the three generated rewrite sets, and corpus noise reduces final retrieval quality even when rewriting is used. Because the benchmark contains 26 cases, one changed case corresponds to approximately 3.8 percentage points. The results are therefore interpreted descriptively rather than as precise population estimates.

\subsection{Effect of Query Rewriting}

Across all three rewrite sets, every main aggregate metric improves over the corresponding original-query baseline in both corpora. Averaged across the three rewrites, strict Hit@1 increases by 19.2 percentage points in both corpus conditions. Strict Hit@3 increases by 17.9 points in the polished corpus and by 25.6 points in the noisy corpus. Strict MRR increases by 0.212 and 0.237, respectively.

The same direction appears under the lenient interpretation. Mean gains are 23.1 points for Hit@1, 12.8 points for Hit@3, and 0.203 MRR in the polished corpus; in the noisy corpus they are 17.9 points, 17.9 points, and 0.188 MRR. The improvement is therefore not limited to a single relevance threshold or one favourable rewrite generation.

Figure~\ref{fig:rewrite-effect-main-metrics} will visualise the original baseline against the mean and range of the three rewrite runs. The most important reading is not that every query improves, but that all six aggregate ranking measures move in the same favourable direction in both corpus environments.

Rewriting recovers part of the loss caused by lexical competition, but the final noisy-corpus scores remain lower.

\subsection{Rewrite Stability}

The three rewrite sets produce the same overall conclusion, but not identical rankings. In the polished corpus, strict Hit@3 ranges from 61.5\% to 73.1\%, with a population standard deviation of 4.8 percentage points; strict MRR ranges from 0.474 to 0.532, with a standard deviation of 0.024. The corresponding noisy-corpus variation is larger: strict Hit@3 ranges from 46.2\% to 61.5\%, and strict MRR from 0.321 to 0.436.

Case-level agreement supports the same interpretation. Under the strict policy, all three rewrites produce the same improved/same/worsened outcome for 19 of 26 polished cases and 18 of 26 noisy cases. Under the lenient policy, agreement rises to 24 of 26 polished cases and 21 of 26 noisy cases. No case is unanimously worsened under the strict policy.

Figure~\ref{fig:rewrite-stability} will show the three rewrite runs separately. The cleanest stability result is the polished lenient condition: most questions either improve or remain unchanged across all three generations. The noisy corpus is less stable because more competing documents can react differently to small changes in augmentation terms.

These results indicate directional robustness rather than determinism. The rewrite model can choose different but plausible institutional terms, and those terms sometimes change the ordering of close BM25 candidates. Repeated generation is therefore useful for estimating sensitivity, even though the core conclusion remains stable.

\subsection{Effect of Corpus Noise}

Corpus noise has a substantial negative effect on absolute retrieval quality. Averaged across the three rewrite sets, polished-corpus strict Hit@1 is 34.6\%, compared with 23.1\% in the noisy corpus. Strict Hit@3 is 67.9\% versus 52.6\%, and strict MRR is 0.500 versus 0.365. The same pattern holds for the lenient metrics: polished retrieval reaches 65.4\% Hit@1, 85.9\% Hit@3, and 0.754 MRR, while the noisy condition reaches 52.6\%, 75.6\%, and 0.630.

The original-query baseline is also strongly affected by noise. In strict Hit@3, the polished baseline exceeds the noisy baseline by 23.1 percentage points. After rewriting, the mean gap falls to 15.4 points. This suggests that rewriting partially mitigates lexical competition, but does not eliminate the importance of corpus curation and retrieval-side controls.

\subsection{Additional Context Metrics}

Hit@3 remains the primary answerability metric. The extra-context measures answer a narrower question: after one useful source has already been found, does the top-three window contain additional useful evidence?

These metrics require particular caution in the polished corpus. That corpus was curated around one intended primary source per benchmark question. Additional useful hits can still occur because a document selected for one question may also help answer another, but the corpus was not designed to contain multiple complementary sources for every case. Extra-context gain in the polished condition is therefore supplementary and partly reflects corpus construction rather than an independent retrieval objective.

The noisy corpus has slightly higher mean ExtraCoverage@3 than the polished corpus, 57.1\% versus 55.3\%. This does not imply better retrieval. The denominator contains only answerable cases, which are fewer under noise. Moreover, mean gain per answerable case and gain when present are lower in the noisy corpus. ExtraCoverage@3 must therefore be read together with Hit@3 and the two gain measures, not in isolation.

\section{Discussion and Error Analysis}

The aggregate and qualitative results point to a coherent mechanism. Original student questions often contain generic conversational structures---for example, \emph{come posso}, \emph{quali sono}, or broad terms such as \emph{presentazione} and \emph{disponibile}. In a heterogeneous institutional corpus, these expressions match many unrelated FAQ headings and body passages. BM25 can score such overlap highly even when the document does not address the intended procedure.

Useful rewrites reduce that generic structure and introduce a small number of institutional concepts, such as study-plan compilation, host organisations, certificate recognition, or scholarship eligibility. This improves lexical alignment without replacing the deterministic retriever. The qualitative cases in Section~\ref{sec:qualitative-case-analysis} provide concrete examples of this effect.

The remaining failures fall into several classes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lexical mismatch}: the rewritten query still lacks the terminology used by the target source;
    \item \textbf{Rewrite drift}: augmentation introduces a neighbouring administrative intent or an unsupported assumption;
    \item \textbf{Generic-language collision}: common conversational expressions match unrelated FAQs;
    \item \textbf{Missing source}: the correct official document is absent from the selected corpus;
    \item \textbf{Corpus noise}: additional unrelated documents compete with the intended source;
    \item \textbf{Granularity or field-weighting issue}: the right document is indexed, but its relevant segment is not competitive;
    \item \textbf{Duplicate or yearly versions}: similar documents compete for the same ranking positions.
\end{itemize}

The polished/noisy comparison shows that query rewriting and corpus design solve different problems. Rewriting improves the lexical representation of the information need. Corpus curation, duplicate handling, segmentation, and field weighting determine which candidate documents compete and how strongly. A practical system needs both.

\section{Product Context}

The thesis is extracted from a broader prototype for a university bureaucracy assistant. The complete system includes controlled acquisition, manifest and revision tracking, HTML/PDF extraction, OpenSearch BM25 retrieval, backend APIs, a frontend interface, and debugging tools.

The thesis isolates retrieval because correct source selection is a necessary condition for grounded answer generation. Fluent generation cannot compensate for missing or irrelevant evidence.

\section{Limitations}

The benchmark contains 26 manually curated questions, so the results should not be interpreted as a comprehensive estimate for all university bureaucracy queries. With this sample size, one case changes an aggregate Hit metric by approximately 3.8 percentage points.

Judgements were produced by one evaluator. Although the same query--segment pair was labelled consistently across runs, independent annotation would be needed to estimate inter-rater agreement and reduce subjective bias.

\section{Conclusions}

The research question asks whether a low-cost LLM rewrite layer can meaningfully improve BM25 retrieval over official bureaucratic sources. Within the limits of this benchmark, the answer is positive.

All three frozen rewrite sets improve every main aggregate retrieval metric over the original-query baseline in both corpus conditions. The gain persists in the noisy corpus, showing that rewriting remains useful when relevant documents compete with substantially more unrelated material. At the same time, the noisy-corpus results remain below the polished-corpus results, confirming that query rewriting does not replace corpus quality and retrieval engineering.

The repeated runs also show that the effect is reasonably stable but not deterministic. Most cases have the same outcome across all three rewrites, especially under the lenient policy in the polished corpus, while the noisy corpus is more sensitive to term-selection differences.

The central contribution is therefore both architectural and empirical: a reproducible retrieval pipeline, a controlled original-versus-rewritten benchmark, and evidence that a thin LLM layer can bridge part of the lexical gap between student language and institutional terminology without replacing deterministic BM25 retrieval.

\section{Future Work}

Future work may include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item an Italian stemmed or analysed fallback field while retaining exact-field boosts;
    \item hybrid lexical and vector retrieval;
    \item reranking of the BM25 candidate set;
    \item larger and independently judged evaluation datasets;
    \item automatic checks for rewrite drift;
    \item broader source coverage and stronger yearly-document handling;
    \item full assistant evaluation, including answer correctness, citations, refusals, and user studies.
\end{itemize}
