\chapter*{Summary}
\label{sommario}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Summary}

University bureaucratic information is distributed across official web pages, programme pages, regulations, FAQs, and yearly PDF documents. Students describe their needs in informal language, while institutional sources often use formal administrative terminology. This lexical mismatch can reduce the effectiveness of BM25 retrieval even when the intended answer is present in the corpus.

This thesis investigates whether a low-cost Large Language Model can act as a lightweight query-rewriting layer that improves BM25 retrieval over official UniTrento bureaucratic sources. The LLM is not evaluated as the final answer generator. Instead, it produces a two-layer search query composed of a cleaned version of the student's wording and a small augmentation with institutional terms.

The benchmark contains 26 Italian student-style questions. Original and rewritten queries are evaluated on two overlapping corpus views: a curated polished corpus and a substantially broader noisy corpus. Three independently generated rewrite sets are frozen and reused unchanged on both corpora, allowing rewrite variability and corpus noise to be analysed separately. The top three distinct documents are manually labelled as relevant, partially relevant, or irrelevant, and evaluated through strict and lenient Hit@1, Hit@3, and Mean Reciprocal Rank.

Across all three rewrite sets, rewriting improves every main aggregate retrieval metric in both corpus conditions. Averaged over the three rewrites, strict Hit@3 improves by 17.9 percentage points in the polished corpus and by 25.6 points in the noisy corpus. However, final rewritten performance remains lower under noise: mean strict Hit@3 is 67.9\% in the polished corpus and 52.6\% in the noisy corpus. The repeated runs show moderate variability but preserve the same overall conclusion, with stronger case-level agreement in the polished corpus.

The results support the use of the LLM as a thin semantic layer over deterministic retrieval. Rewriting can bridge part of the lexical gap between student language and institutional terminology, but it does not replace corpus quality, segmentation, duplicate handling, or ranking design. The broader prototype supplies the acquisition, extraction, indexing, backend, frontend, and debugging infrastructure needed to make the experiment reproducible and inspectable.
